# Core Philosophy

## The Formula

**Experience + Story + Discovery + Connection + Value.**

Every piece of content The Link makes should be traceable back to this formula. If a script, a clip, or a marketing idea can't point to at least a couple of these elements, it probably isn't a The Link piece of content yet.

## The Central Question

We are not primarily asking guests:

> "What service do you sell?"

We are asking:

> "What have you learned from doing this for years?"

That single shift in question changes everything downstream — the interview questions, the editing choices, the marketing copy, and what counts as a good episode.

## What We're Actually Making

The project reveals the human being behind the business. We want real stories, real experience, real expertise, and real personality — not a polished sales pitch wearing a documentary format.

The tone is:

- Curious
- Intelligent
- Friendly
- Slightly comedic
- Human
- Practical
- Professional without being corporate
- Entertaining without becoming fake
- Informative without becoming a lecture

## The Two Feelings We're Designing For

**The audience should feel:** "I came here because I was curious, and I left knowing something I didn't know before."

**The guest should feel:** "This allowed people to understand what I actually know and do."

If an episode achieves the guest's feeling but not the audience's, it's a corporate testimonial. If it achieves the audience's feeling but not the guest's, it risks feeling exploitative or shallow. Both must be true.

## Value Before Views

The project should prioritize giving the viewer something real over chasing raw attention. But this is not an excuse for content to be boring or badly built for distribution — the content still has to be intelligently designed for attention, retention, and social sharing. Value and craft are not in tension; the craft is what gets the value seen.

A useful internal test for every episode: **"What did I learn that I didn't know before watching this?"** If nobody on the team can answer that in one sentence, the episode isn't ready.

## Indirect Marketing, By Design

The business may benefit from exposure, but the promotion must always be indirect and natural. This is not a soft suggestion — it is a structural rule that shapes the entire interview and edit.

We do **not** want episodes that feel like:

> "Go visit this garage. They are the best."

We want the viewer to discover, on their own, over the course of the episode, that:

> "This person specializes in exactly this thing. If I ever need that expertise, now I know where to look."

The difference between those two sentences is the difference between an ad and The Link. See `04_DECISION_PRINCIPLES.md` for how to test any piece of content against this line.

## Location as Storytelling

Episodes happen at the guest's actual place of work — a garage, a workshop, a studio, a facility, a collection. The environment is not a backdrop; it is part of the story. The audience should feel like they're being taken somewhere they normally couldn't access.

## Content as a Mine, Not a Deliverable

A single recorded session is the raw material for an entire content ecosystem — a full episode, a YouTube upload, and a wide spread of shorts, clips, quotes, and stills. The production system is built around content *extraction*, not just episode production. Full detail in `02_CONTENT_STRATEGY/05_CONTENT_REPURPOSING.md`.

## Community, Not Just Audience

The long-term goal is bigger than view count. It's a community that participates — suggesting guests, asking questions, showing up to lives, and eventually forming a real network connecting people to people, expertise to people, and people to opportunities. Full detail in `07_COMMUNITY/01_COMMUNITY_STRATEGY.md`.
